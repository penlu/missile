\title{Optimal pursuit by a missile of an evading spacecraft}
\author{penlu \and grumpyoldman}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
  We study a pursuit--evasion differential game in free space between a
  missile with acceleration bound $A$ and a spacecraft with acceleration bound
  $\beta A$, $\beta < 1$, in which the missile seeks point capture at minimum
  total delta-V.  We show that the missile can always force capture with
  finite delta-V (even if its feedback law is announced in advance), derive
  the stationary Hamilton--Jacobi--Isaacs equation characterizing the game
  value, identify the pointwise saddle structure of the optimal controls
  (including a nonempty ``coast'' region in which the optimal missile thrust
  is zero), and use the symmetries of the problem to reduce the value to a
  function of two dimensionless variables.  We also revisit the classical
  ``acceleration matching'' guidance policy: we verify its range envelope
  $D^* = v^2/4a$,   \jnote{$D^*$, $v$, and $a$
    weren't defined} exhibit a longitudinal-burn refinement that dominates it
  from every off-nominal state, and explain why the two coincide exactly on
  the nominal boost--terminal split.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}
\newcommand{\R}{\mathbb{R}}

Consider a pursuing missile $P$ and an evading spacecraft $E$ in free space.  We
will consider their future trajectories $\vec{x}_P(t), \vec{x}_E(t) \in \R^3$ for $t \ge 0$,
given initial conditions $\vec{x}_P(0)$, $\vec{x}_E(0)$, $\dot{\vec{x}}_P(0)$, and $\dot{\vec{x}}_E(0)$.

The missile and spacecraft can dynamically choose any twice-differentiable trajectories
subject to the constraints \begin{equation}
  \|
  \ddot{\vec{x}}_P(t) \| \le A
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \|
  \ddot{\vec{x}}_E(t) \| \le \beta A,
\end{equation}
where $0 \le \beta < 1$ is a fixed parameter.  \jnote{this seems to implicitly
  assume that the evader would have a larger delta-v budget to balance that it
  is more acceleration-constrained.  is this realistic in any space scenario,
  futuristic or otherwise?}  To be more precise, the missile
and spacecraft strategies are functions $\vec{a}_P, \vec{a}_E$ that take as
input $\vec{x}_P(t)$, $\vec{x}_E(t)$, $\dot{\vec{x}}_P(t)$,
$\dot{\vec{x}}_E(t)$, and output a vector in $\R^3$ bounded in norm by $A$ or
$ \beta A$.  Once $\vec{a}_P$ and $\vec{a}_E$ are chosen, the trajectories are
then defined as the solution to the ODEs
\begin{align}
  \ddot{\vec{x}}_P(t) &= \vec{a}_P \big (\vec{x}_P(t), \vec{x}_E(t),
                        \dot{\vec{x}}_P(t), \dot{\vec{x}}_E(t) \big )\\
  \ddot{\vec{x}}_E(t) &= \vec{a}_E\big (\vec{x}_P(t), \vec{x}_E(t),
                        \dot{\vec{x}}_P(t), \dot{\vec{x}}_E(t) \big ).
\end{align}
(\jnote{I don't really know what the following means; probably deserves
  more discussion as a proper subsection that delves into nuances about the
  problem modeling.  For example, what are settings in which discontinuous
  feedback laws would make sense?  does it make sense here?  Does making the
  capture radius positive change anything?} For discontinuous feedback laws the closed-loop ODE need not admit classical
solutions; a fully rigorous treatment would use Filippov solutions and/or
nonanticipative strategies in the sense of Elliott--Kalton
\cite{evanssouganidis1984,bardi1997}.  We work at the level of feedback laws
and do not belabor this point.)

The objectives of the missile, in order of decreasing priority, are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item To achieve $\vec{x}_P(T) = \vec{x}_E(T)$
for some $T \ge 0$ (``capture'').
\item To achieve (1) with minimal ``delta-V''
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:missile-delta-v}
  \Delta v_P \eqdef \int_{0}^T \big \| \ddot{\vec{x}}_P(t) \big \| dt.
\end{equation}
\item To achieve (1) with minimal capture time $T$.
\end{enumerate}
The objectives of the spacecraft are exactly the opposite: maximize $\Delta v_P$, and among all
maximizing trajectories, maximize $T$.  (Our assumption
that $\beta < 1$ makes (1) attainable with finite $\Delta v_P$ no matter how the
spacecraft evades; see \cref{thm:finite-dv}.  We denote by
$V$ the value of the primary game, i.e., the least $\Delta v_P$ the missile can
guarantee; the time objectives serve only to break ties among
$\Delta v_P$-optimal strategies.)

We wish to understand optimal pursuit and evasion strategies, their resulting
trajectories, and their costs.  Dually, we wish to understand the ``missile
capture'' envelope -- the set of initial conditions under which the missile can
assure (1) occurs while subjecting $\Delta v_P$ to a fixed bound.

Throughout we work in relative coordinates
\begin{equation}
  \vec{r} \eqdef \vec{x}_E - \vec{x}_P, \qquad
  \vec{v} \eqdef \dot{\vec{x}}_E - \dot{\vec{x}}_P, \qquad
  \ddot{\vec{r}} = \vec{a}_E - \vec{a}_P,
\end{equation}
\jnote{should either use $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{a}$ or $\dot{\vec{r}}$ and
  $\ddot{\vec{r}}$ (I prefer the former).}
and write $\rho \eqdef \norm{\vec{r}}$, $\hat{\vec{r}} \eqdef \vec{r}/\rho$.
Capture is the event $\vec{r}(T) = \vec{0}$.  \Cref{sec:symmetries} justifies
that $V$ depends only on $(\vec r, \vec v)$, indeed only on three scalars
derived from them.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Differential games.}
Pursuit--evasion differential games originate with Isaacs \cite{isaacs1965},
who introduced the HJI equation and the notion of the usable part of the
target set, and solved a catalogue of pursuit problems including the
\emph{isotropic rocket}: an acceleration-steered pursuer against a
simple-motion (velocity-controlled) evader, with time-to-capture as the
payoff and a positive capture radius.  \jnote{this is supposed to model a
  missile chasing an aircraft, right?  would be good to motivate why our variant
of the pursuit game might be motivated in real life} Our game differs in all three
respects: both players are acceleration-controlled double integrators,
capture is exact point coincidence, and the payoff is the pursuer's fuel.
Rigorous notions of value for such games were later developed via
nonanticipative strategies  and viscosity solutions \jnote{what are these?}
\cite{crandalllions1983,evanssouganidis1984,bardi1997}, and independently in
the positional (feedback) framework of Krasovskii and Subbotin
\cite{krasovskii1988}.  Since our dynamics are linear, the pure capture
question (the ``game of kind'') also falls within the scope of Pontryagin's
theory of linear differential games \cite{pontryagin1967}.  Pursuit games in
which the controls are subject to integral (energy-type) constraints are
likewise classical (see \cite{krasovskii1988} and references); there, fuel
enters as a hard constraint on the admissible controls, whereas here it is
the payoff itself.  \jnote{is this not equivalent?}

\paragraph{Missile guidance.}
In the guidance literature, pursuit--evasion first appears in
linear-quadratic form \cite{ho1965}, where quadratic penalties \jnote{why?} on both
players' accelerations yield proportional-navigation-type feedback laws.
Hard acceleration bounds were introduced by Gutman and Leitmann
\cite{gutmanleitmann1976} and developed extensively by Shinar
\cite{shinar1981}: those games linearize the kinematics about an initial
collision course, take terminal miss distance (or capture-or-not) as the
payoff over a short terminal phase, and yield bang-bang saddle-point
strategies.  Zero-effort-miss guidance and its variants are treated at
length in \cite{zarchan2012}.  In all of these, fuel is at most an implicit
consideration (or a quadratic proxy); our game instead makes delta-V the
explicit objective over the entire engagement, which is what produces the
burn/coast structure absent from time- and miss-distance games.

\paragraph{Fuel-optimal spaceflight.}
Minimum-fuel trajectory optimization against a \emph{non-maneuvering} target
is a pillar of astrodynamics: Lawden's primer vector theory \cite{lawden1963}
characterizes fuel-optimal trajectories by an adjoint (``primer'') vector,
with maximum-thrust arcs where its norm exceeds $1$ and coast arcs where it
is below $1$.  The costate $\vec\lambda = \pdv*{V}{\vec v}$ of
\cref{sec:equilibrium} is precisely the game-theoretic analogue of the
primer vector, and the free boundary $\norm{\vec\lambda} = 1$ of
\cref{sec:defining} is Lawden's switching condition generalized to an
optimally maneuvering target.  \jnote{I would welcome a full section giving a
  primer (no pun intended) on the primer vector theory and its relation to
  pursuit-evasion} Orbital pursuit--evasion games including
gravity have been solved numerically \cite{pontani2009}, but again with
time-type payoffs; our free-space model deliberately strips out gravity to
isolate the fuel-versus-maneuver tradeoff.  Numerical solution of HJI
equations of the kind derived in \cref{sec:hji} is well developed via
level-set and semi-Lagrangian methods \cite{mitchell2005,bardi1997}.

\paragraph{Positioning and novelty.}
The acceleration-matching policy of \cref{sec:matching} is, as far as we know,
folklore: it circulates in informal analyses of ``realistic'' space
combat\footnote{E.g., in discussions surrounding the simulation game
  \emph{Children of a Dead Earth} and the \emph{Atomic Rockets} reference site.}
rather than in the academic literature, and we are not aware of a published
analysis of its optimality.  More broadly, we have not found a prior treatment
of the specific game studied here: both players ideal double integrators in free
space, hard acceleration bounds with advantage ratio $\beta < 1$, exact point
capture, and the pursuer's delta-V \cref{eq:missile-delta-v} as the
(lexicographically primary) payoff.  The closest threads each miss one
ingredient: the bounded-control terminal-phase games have the hard bounds but a
miss-distance payoff and linearized kinematics; primer vector theory has the
fuel payoff but no adversary; integral-constraint pursuit treats fuel as a
constraint rather than a payoff.  Accordingly, we believe the following are new,
while emphasizing that each is assembled from standard parts: the finite-delta-V
feedback strategy and the $\sqrt{A\rho}$ capture-cost scaling of
\cref{thm:finite-dv}; the identification of the burn/coast free boundary as an
adversarial primer vector condition (\cref{sec:equilibrium,sec:coast}); the
reduction of the game to the two-variable PDE \cref{eq:g-pde}; and the
domination analysis of acceleration matching in \cref{sec:lateral-suboptimal},
including the observation that the classical range envelope is nevertheless
exact on the nominal boost--terminal split.  We make no strong priority claims:
the pursuit-games literature is vast and scattered, and we would welcome
pointers to prior appearances of any of these observations.  \jnote{too
  academically sloppy to claim inability to do a proper literature search}

\section{Existence of a Finite $\Delta v$ Strategy}
\label{sec:finite-dv}

We claim that even if the missile must commit to its (state-feedback)
acceleration law before the spaceship chooses its own, the missile has a
winning strategy whenever $\beta < 1$.  The underlying mechanism is that the
missile's acceleration disk strictly contains the spacecraft's: by thrusting at
full $A$ in a direction $\hat{\vec u}$ of its choice, the missile guarantees a
net relative acceleration of magnitude at least $(1-\beta)A$ along
$-\hat{\vec u}$, no matter what the spacecraft does.  The missile can therefore
steer the \emph{relative velocity} like a one-dimensional double integrator
with authority $(1-\beta)A$, and excess acceleration can then be used to
produce closure.

\begin{lemma}[directional dominance]
  \crefalias{theorem}{lemma}\label{lem:dominance}
  Fix a unit vector $\hat{\vec u}$ and suppose the missile plays
  $\vec a_P = A \hat{\vec u}$.  Then for every admissible spacecraft control,
  \[
    \dv{t}\big(\vec v \cdot \hat{\vec u}\big)
    = (\vec a_E - \vec a_P)\cdot \hat{\vec u}
    \le \beta A - A = -(1-\beta) A .
  \]
\end{lemma}

\begin{theorem}
  \label{thm:finite-dv}
  There is an absolute constant $C$ such that for every $\beta \in [0,1)$ and
  every initial relative state $(\vec r, \vec v)$, the missile has a
  state-feedback strategy -- which may even be announced to the spacecraft in
  advance -- that guarantees capture with
  \[
    \Delta v_P \;\le\; \frac{C}{1-\beta}\Big( \norm{\vec v} + \sqrt{A \rho}
    \Big),
    \qquad
    T \;\le\; \frac{C}{1-\beta}\Big( \frac{\norm{\vec v}}{A} +
    \sqrt{\rho / A} \Big).
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  The strategy has two phases.

  \emph{Phase 1 (kill the relative velocity).}  While $\vec v \ne \vec 0$ the
  missile plays $\vec a_P = A \hat{\vec v}$.  By \cref{lem:dominance} with
  $\hat{\vec u} = \hat{\vec v}$,
  $\dv*{\norm{\vec v}}{t} \le -(1-\beta)A$, so $\vec v$ is driven to $\vec 0$
  within time $t_1 \le \norm{\vec v(0)} / ((1-\beta)A)$, at delta-V cost
  $A t_1 \le \norm{\vec v(0)}/(1-\beta)$.  Meanwhile the separation grows by at
  most $\norm{\vec v(0)} t_1 \le \norm{\vec v(0)}^2/((1-\beta)A)$.%
  \footnote{$\hat{\vec v}$ is undefined at $\vec v = \vec 0$; since
    $\norm{\vec v}$ decreases at a rate bounded away from zero, $\vec v = 0$ is
    reached in finite time and the strategy switches to Phase 2.  Chattering
    concerns can be handled with a standard hysteresis band.}

  \emph{Phase 2 (guarded dash).}  Now $\vec v \approx \vec 0$ at some
  separation $d$.  Let $\vec h \eqdef \vec r \times \vec v$ be the relative
  angular momentum (per unit mass); note $\dot{\vec h} = \vec r \times
  (\vec a_E - \vec a_P)$, so the spacecraft can grow $\norm{\vec h}$ at rate at
  most $\rho \beta A$, while the missile, by devoting transverse thrust
  authority $\beta A + \eta$ (for a small margin $\eta > 0$) against
  $\hat{\vec h}$, can always drive $\norm{\vec h}$ back toward $0$ at net rate
  $\rho\eta$.  The missile runs this transverse containment loop with a
  deadband, so that $\norm{\vec h} \approx 0$ and hence the tangential relative
  speed $\tau = \norm{\vec h}/\rho$ stays negligible: the engagement remains
  effectively collinear, and the centrifugal and Coriolis terms of
  \cref{sec:hji} are negligible.

  The remaining thrust authority is applied along the (essentially fixed) line
  of sight.  The key accounting point is that the spacecraft's transverse and
  radial efforts draw on a common budget: if it spends $e_\perp$ transversely
  and $e_r$ radially, then $e_\perp^2 + e_r^2 \le (\beta A)^2$.  Since the
  missile's containment loop only spends transverse thrust
  $\approx e_\perp + \eta$ when the spacecraft does, the missile's leftover
  longitudinal authority $\sqrt{A^2 - (e_\perp + \eta)^2}$ always exceeds the
  spacecraft's radial effort $\sqrt{(\beta A)^2 - e_\perp^2}$ by at least
  $(1-\beta)A - O(\eta)$, uniformly over the spacecraft's split.  The
  longitudinal channel is thus a one-dimensional pursuit with guaranteed net
  closing authority $\gtrsim (1-\beta)A$: the missile boosts to closing speed
  $\sigma^* \asymp \sqrt{(1-\beta) A d}$, tops up as needed against radial
  evasion, and reaches $\rho = 0$ in time $O\big(\sqrt{d/((1-\beta)A)}\big)$.
  Its Phase-2 delta-V is at most the boost cost
  $O\big(\sigma^*/(1-\beta)\big)$ plus the containment and top-up costs, which
  run at rate $O(\beta A)$ for the duration; all terms are
  $O\big(\sqrt{Ad}/(1-\beta)\big)$.  Combining the two phases (and absorbing
  the Phase-1 separation drift into $d$) gives the stated bounds.  We have not
  attempted to optimize the constant or the dependence on $(1-\beta)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
  The $\norm{\vec v} + \sqrt{A\rho}$ scaling is exactly what dimensional
  analysis predicts (\cref{sec:symmetries}): from a distance $\rho$ at rest, the
  only velocity scale constructible from $\rho$ and $A$ is $\sqrt{A\rho}$.
  \Cref{sec:matching} shows the sharper upper bound $2\sqrt{\beta A \rho}$ from
  rest, whose $\beta \to 0$ behavior is much better than the crude bound above.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
  The strategy above uses only the current state, and its guarantees are
  worst-case over spacecraft behavior; in particular it retains its guarantee
  when the spacecraft knows the missile's law (a Stackelberg setting favoring
  the evader).  Consequently the game has a well-defined finite value $V$, and
  upper bounds proved by exhibiting missile strategies are legitimate bounds on
  $V$.
\end{remark}

\section{Existence of a Pure Strategy Saddle Point in Each State}
\label{sec:equilibrium}

In this section we observe that at any game state where $V$ is differentiable
(see \cref{sec:regularity}), the instantaneous game of choosing accelerations
has a pure-strategy saddle point: neither missile nor spaceship is
incentivized to deviate unilaterally.  Define the (dimensionless\footnote{$V$
  has units of velocity and we differentiate with respect to a velocity.})
costate
\[
  \vec\lambda \eqdef \pdv{V}{\vec v} ,
\]
where $V = V(\vec r, \vec v)$ is the game value in relative coordinates.  The
instantaneous Hamiltonian (cf.\ \cref{sec:hji}) is
\[
  H(\vec a_P, \vec a_E)
  = \pdv{V}{\vec r} \cdot \vec v
  + \vec\lambda \cdot (\vec a_E - \vec a_P)
  + \norm{\vec a_P},
\]
which is \emph{additively separable} in the two controls; hence
$\min_{\vec a_P} \max_{\vec a_E} H = \max_{\vec a_E} \min_{\vec a_P} H$
pointwise (Isaacs' minimax condition holds automatically), and the pointwise
optimizers form a saddle point.  They are:
\begin{itemize}
\item the spaceship accelerates at full throttle along the costate,
  $\vec a_E^* = \beta A \hat{\vec\lambda}$ (any direction if
  $\vec\lambda = \vec 0$);
\item the missile
  \begin{itemize}
  \item accelerates at full throttle along the costate,
    $\vec a_P^* = A \hat{\vec\lambda}$, if $\norm{\vec\lambda} > 1$;
  \item does not accelerate at all if $\norm{\vec\lambda} < 1$;
  \item accelerates by any amount $c\,\hat{\vec\lambda}$, $c \in [0, A]$, if
    $\norm{\vec\lambda} = 1$ (the secondary time objective breaks this tie in
    favor of burning).
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
The interpretation is a marginal cost--benefit comparison: a unit of missile
delta-V buys a unit change of relative velocity against the costate, worth
$\norm{\vec\lambda}$ units of $V$; the missile burns exactly when the marginal
value exceeds the marginal cost $1$.  Readers familiar with astrodynamics
will recognize $\vec\lambda$ as the primer vector \cite{lawden1963} of the
engagement, and the rule above as Lawden's burn/coast switching condition,
here arising in a game rather than in open-loop optimal control
(cf.\ \cref{sec:related}).

Two remarks on the structure.  First, note that \emph{both} players thrust in
the same direction $\hat{\vec\lambda}$: because the missile's thrust enters
the relative dynamics $\ddot{\vec r} = \vec a_E - \vec a_P$ with a minus sign,
parallel physical thrusts have opposing effects on the relative state.  (In
the prototypical tail chase, $\vec\lambda$ has a large component along
$\hat{\vec r}$: the spacecraft burns away from the missile while the missile
burns toward the spacecraft -- the same absolute direction.)  Second, the
saddle point is unique except in the degenerate cases $\vec\lambda = \vec 0$
(spaceship indifferent) and $\norm{\vec\lambda} = 1$ (missile indifferent);
the locus $\norm{\vec\lambda} = 1$ is the free boundary between the missile's
``burn'' and ``coast'' regions (\cref{sec:coast,sec:defining}).

Along saddle-point play, substituting $\vec a_P^*, \vec a_E^*$ into the
defining equation of \cref{sec:defining} gives
$\dv*{V}{t} = -\norm{\vec a_P^*}$: the value decreases exactly at the rate the
missile spends delta-V, as it must.

\section{Regularity of the Game Value}
\label{sec:regularity}

\jnote{this should be a ``claude note'' analogous to the jnote and enote author
  notes} The original draft of this section claimed that $V$ is everywhere
differentiable.  That is false as stated: $V$ fails to be even
\emph{continuous} at the capture set, except on its ``usable part'' in the
sense of Isaacs \cite{isaacs1965}.  We record the correct statements.

\begin{proposition}[discontinuity on the non-usable boundary]
  \crefalias{theorem}{proposition}\label{prop:discont}
  Fix $\vec v$ with $s \eqdef \vec v \cdot \hat{\vec r} > 0$ (receding).  Then
  for all $\rho > 0$,
  \[
    V(\rho \hat{\vec r}, \vec v) \ \ge\ s + \sqrt{2 \beta A \rho} ,
  \]
  whereas $V(\vec 0, \vec v) = 0$.  Hence $V$ does not extend continuously to
  receding states on the capture set.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Let the spacecraft simply burn $\beta A \hat{\vec u}$ along the fixed initial
  line-of-sight direction $\hat{\vec u} = \hat{\vec r}(0)$, and let
  $\xi = \vec r \cdot \hat{\vec u}$.  Capture at time $T$ requires $\xi(T) \le 0$,
  and
  \[
    0 \ge \xi(T) = \rho + s T + \tfrac{1}{2}\beta A T^2
    - \int_0^T (T - t)\, \vec a_P \cdot \hat{\vec u} \, dt
    \ \ge\ \rho + s T + \tfrac{1}{2}\beta A T^2 - T \Delta v_P ,
  \]
  so $\Delta v_P \ge s + \rho/T + \tfrac{1}{2}\beta A T \ge s + \sqrt{2\beta
    A \rho}$, minimizing over $T$.
\end{proof}

A similar barrier exists for ``flyby'' approaches: if $\vec r \to \vec 0$ with
$\vec v$ transverse to $\vec r$ (large tangential speed $\tau$, small
time-to-go), converting the flyby into a hit costs a transverse velocity
change on the order of $\tau$, so $V \not\to 0$ there either.  The states from
which $V \to 0$ as $\rho \to 0$ are precisely the near-collision-course
states, with $\vec v$ closing along $-\hat{\vec r}$ and small tangential
component: this is the usable part of the target set, and it is the only place
where the boundary condition $V = 0$ may be imposed
(\cref{sec:defining}).  (The standard alternative is to give the missile a
positive capture radius $\ell$, which restores continuity up to the target set
and recovers our game in the limit $\ell \to 0$.)

Away from the capture set the value is well behaved.  \Cref{thm:finite-dv}
provides a modulus of continuity by strategy adaptation: from a perturbed
state $(\vec r + \delta\vec r, \vec v + \delta\vec v)$, the missile can first
steer the perturbation to zero relative to a shadow trajectory that plays
optimally from $(\vec r, \vec v)$ -- by \cref{thm:finite-dv} applied to the
difference system this costs
$O\big((\norm{\delta \vec v} + \sqrt{A \norm{\delta \vec r}})/(1-\beta)\big)$
-- and then follow the shadow's optimal play.  Hence, locally uniformly on
$\{\rho > 0\}$,
\[
  \abs{V(\vec r + \delta \vec r, \vec v + \delta \vec v) - V(\vec r, \vec v)}
  \ \le\ \frac{C}{1-\beta} \Big( \norm{\delta \vec v} + \sqrt{A
    \norm{\delta\vec r}} \Big):
\]
$V$ is Lipschitz in velocity and H\"older-$\tfrac12$ in position.  We expect
(but do not prove) that $V$ is in fact $C^1$ off a lower-dimensional union of
singular surfaces (switching and dispersal surfaces), as is typical in
differential games; across such surfaces the gradient may jump.  Accordingly,
all gradient-based statements in this paper (\cref{sec:equilibrium,sec:defining})
should be read as holding where $V$ is differentiable, and the defining PDE
should be interpreted globally in the viscosity sense
\cite{crandalllions1983,evanssouganidis1984,bardi1997}.

\section{Missile Max-Burn is NOT WLOG}
\label{sec:coast}

It is tempting to assume that the missile should always thrust at full
throttle (as is WLOG in the pure time-optimal game).  For the delta-V
objective this is false: the state space contains a nonempty \emph{coast
  region} -- namely $\{\norm{\vec\lambda} < 1\}$ in the language of
\cref{sec:equilibrium} -- in which the optimal missile thrust is zero.

\begin{proposition}
  \crefalias{theorem}{proposition}\label{prop:coast}
  Consider collision-course states: $\vec v = -\sigma_0 \hat{\vec r}$ with
  closing speed $\sigma_0 > 0$, separation $\rho$, and time-to-go
  $t_{go} = \rho / \sigma_0$.  In the regime
  $\sigma_0 \gg \max\big(\sqrt{A\rho},\, \beta A t_{go}\big)$:
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item $V \le \beta A t_{go} (1 + o(1))$; but
  \item every strategy that always burns at full throttle until capture incurs
    $\Delta v_P \ge A t_{go} (1 - o(1))$.
  \end{enumerate}
  Since $\beta < 1$, max-burn is strictly suboptimal at all sufficiently high
  closing speeds.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  (1) The missile uses the transverse containment loop of
  \cref{thm:finite-dv} (spend rate at most $\beta A + \eta$, and only while
  the spacecraft spends transversely) and ignores radial evasion entirely.
  Radial evasion at $\beta A$ changes the closing speed by at most
  $\beta A t \ll \sigma_0$, so capture occurs at time $t_{go}(1 + o(1))$, for a
  total spend of at most $\beta A t_{go} (1 + o(1))$.

  (2) Under any controls, closure speed can increase at rate at most
  $(1+\beta)A$, so the capture time $t_c$ satisfies
  $\rho \le \sigma_0 t_c + \tfrac12 (1+\beta) A t_c^2$, giving
  $t_c \ge t_{go}\big(1 - O(A\rho/\sigma_0^2)\big) = t_{go}(1 - o(1))$.  A
  max-burn strategy spends $A t_c$.
\end{proof}

Two corollaries of the picture.  First, in the extreme case $\beta = 0$, a
missile on an exact collision course has $V = 0$ and \emph{any} burn is
strictly wasteful.  Second, in terms of the costate: along these
high-closing-speed collision courses $V \approx c_\beta\, \beta A \rho /
\sigma$, so $\norm{\vec\lambda} \approx \abs{\pdv*{V}{\sigma}} \approx
c_\beta\, \beta A \rho / \sigma^2 \to 0$; the coast region contains all states
with $\sigma^2 \gg \beta A \rho$ up to constants, consistent with
$\norm{\vec\lambda} < 1$.  The optimal missile thus \emph{waits}: while
coasting, the spacecraft's maneuvering is exactly offset (in value) by the
shrinking time-to-go, and $V$ is conserved until the trajectory reaches the
free boundary $\norm{\vec\lambda} = 1$, where terminal corrections begin.

\section{Defining Equations for the Game Value}
\label{sec:defining}

$V$ should be determined by the following equations (derived in
\cref{sec:hji}).  Writing $\vec\lambda = \pdv*{V}{\vec v}$, stationarity gives,
at every point of differentiability with $\rho > 0$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:defining}
  \pdv{V}{\vec r} \cdot \vec v
  \;+\; \beta A \norm{\vec \lambda}
  \;-\; A \, \max\big( \norm{\vec\lambda} - 1, \; 0 \big)
  \;=\; 0 .
\end{equation}
Equivalently, in the burn region $\norm{\vec\lambda} \ge 1$,
\[
  \pdv{V}{\vec r} \cdot \vec v + A\Big( 1 - (1 - \beta) \norm{\vec\lambda}
  \Big) = 0,
\]
while in the coast region $\norm{\vec\lambda} \le 1$,
\[
  \pdv{V}{\vec r} \cdot \vec v + \beta A \norm{\vec\lambda} = 0 .
\]
The interface $\norm{\vec\lambda} = 1$ is a free boundary, not known a priori.

The boundary condition is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bc}
  V \to 0 \quad \text{as } \rho \to 0 \text{ along closing, near-collision
    approaches (the usable part of the target set)},
\end{equation}
and \emph{no} boundary condition is imposed on the rest of the capture set: as
shown in \cref{sec:regularity}, $V$ is discontinuous across the non-usable
part, and \cref{eq:defining} should be understood in the viscosity sense, in
which framework one expects a comparison principle to single out $V$ uniquely
\cite{crandalllions1983,bardi1997}.  We do not attempt a uniqueness proof
here.

As sanity checks: \cref{eq:defining} is dimensionally consistent ($V$ has
units of velocity, so $\pdv*{V}{\vec r}\cdot\vec v$ has units of acceleration
and $\vec\lambda$ is dimensionless), and along saddle-point play it yields
$\dv*{V}{t} = -\norm{\vec a_P^*}$ (burn region) and $\dv*{V}{t} = 0$ (coast
region), as required.

\section{Simplification via Symmetries, Nondimensionalization, and Dominated Actions}
\label{sec:symmetries}

The way we have defined the problem above is clearly overparameterized.  First
we observe several symmetries, which are the translation, boost, and rotation
facets of Galilean invariance:
\begin{itemize}
\item Symmetry under fixed translation (absolute position doesn't matter; only the relative
  position $\vec{r} = \vec{x}_E - \vec{x}_P$);
\item Symmetry under constant motion (absolute velocities don't matter; only
  the relative velocity $\vec{v} = \dot{\vec{x}}_E - \dot{\vec{x}}_P$);
\item Isotropy (absolute directions don't matter; only the relative
  configuration of $\vec r$ and $\vec v$, i.e., the scalars $\rho = \norm{\vec
    r}$, $v = \norm{\vec v}$ and the angle
  $\theta = \arccos(\hat{\vec r}\cdot\hat{\vec v})$).
\end{itemize}
Thus the six relative coordinates reduce to three scalars, which we may take
to be
\begin{align*}
  \rho &= \norm{\vec r}, \\
  \sigma &= \vec v \cdot \hat{\vec r} = v \cos\theta
  && \text{(radial velocity; $\sigma < 0$ means closing)}, \\
  \tau &= \norm{\vec v - \sigma \hat{\vec r}} = v \sin\theta \ \ge 0
  && \text{(tangential speed)}.
\end{align*}

There is one further reduction, from dimensional analysis (we confirm it
explicitly in \cref{sec:reduction} from the system dynamics).  The data of the
problem provide only one acceleration scale $A$ (with $\beta$ dimensionless),
and the state provides a length $\rho$ and speeds $\sigma, \tau$.  Up to
dimensionless factors there are exactly two independent ways to build an
acceleration from these, namely $A$ and $v^2/\rho$; equivalently, everything
dimensionless can depend on the state only through
\[
  u \eqdef \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{A \rho}}, \qquad
  w \eqdef \frac{\tau}{\sqrt{A \rho}}
  \qquad \left(\text{or through } \theta \text{ and } \Pi \eqdef
    \frac{v^2}{A\rho} = u^2 + w^2 \right).
\]
This means that the optimal accelerations must take the form
\[
  \vec a = A \, m(u, w; \beta)\, \hat{\vec a}, \qquad m \in [0, 1],
\]
where the unit vector $\hat{\vec a}$ lies in $\mathrm{span}\{\hat{\vec r},
\hat{\vec v}\}$ (see below) at an in-plane angle depending only on $(u, w;
\beta)$.  Likewise, since $V$ has units of velocity, it must scale as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:V-scaling}
  V(\rho, \sigma, \tau) = \sqrt{A \rho}\; g(u, w; \beta)
\end{equation}
for a dimensionless function $g$ of two dimensionless state variables.
Equivalently, the dynamics are invariant under the scaling $t \mapsto \kappa
t$, lengths $\mapsto \kappa^2 \cdot$ lengths (so velocities $\mapsto \kappa
\cdot$ velocities) with $A$ fixed, under which $V(\kappa^2\rho, \kappa\sigma,
\kappa\tau) = \kappa V(\rho,\sigma,\tau)$; \cref{eq:V-scaling} is the general
solution of this homogeneity relation.

\paragraph{In-plane burns are WLOG.}
We also observe that without loss of generality $P$ and $E$ both perform only
``in-plane'' burns, i.e., burns satisfying
$\ddot{\vec{x}}_P \cdot (\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) = 0$ and
$\ddot{\vec{x}}_E \cdot ( \vec{r} \times \vec{v}) = 0$.  This is now easy to
see from the reduction: $V$ depends on $\vec v$ only through $\sigma$ and
$\tau$, and
\[
  \pdv{\sigma}{\vec v} = \hat{\vec r}, \qquad
  \pdv{\tau}{\vec v} = \hat{\vec\tau} \eqdef \frac{\vec v - \sigma \hat{\vec
      r}}{\tau},
\]
so the costate
$\vec\lambda = V_\sigma \hat{\vec r} + V_\tau \hat{\vec\tau}$ always lies in
the instantaneous $(\vec r, \vec v)$-plane.  By \cref{sec:equilibrium} both
players' optimal thrusts are parallel to $\vec\lambda$, hence in-plane.
Equivalently: an out-of-plane thrust component changes the reduced state
$(\rho, \sigma, \tau)$ only at second order in $dt$ (it rotates the plane
without changing the in-plane scalars), while costing delta-V at first order
-- a dominated action.  (When $\tau = 0$ the plane is degenerate and the claim
is vacuous; the engagement is collinear.)

The same conclusion can be reached without gradients by a discrete-time
exchange argument: the differential game is the $dt \to 0$ limit of a discrete
game in which the following steps are cycled indefinitely:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Pursuer sets its own acceleration $\vec{a}_P$ as a function of
  $\vec{x}_P$, $\vec{v}_P$, $\vec{x}_E$, and $\vec{v}_E$.
\item Environment first updates
  $\vec{v}_P \gets \vec{v}_P + \vec{a}_P \cdot dt$ and ``charges'' the pursuer
  $\| \vec{a}_P \| \cdot dt$.  Then, if $\vec{x}_E$ lies on the line segment
  connecting $\vec{x}_P$ and $\vec{x}_P + \vec{v}_P \cdot dt$, the pursuer is
  said to ``capture'' the evader and the game terminates.  Otherwise, the
  environment updates $\vec{x}_P \gets \vec{x}_P + \vec{v}_P \cdot dt$.
\item Evader sets its own acceleration $\vec{a}_E$ as a function of
  $\vec{x}_P$, $\vec{v}_P$, $\vec{x}_E$, and $\vec{v}_E$.
\item Environment ``forward steps'' the positions and velocities of the evader
  by updating
  $\vec{v}_E \gets \vec{v}_E + \vec{a}_E \cdot dt$ and then
  $\vec{x}_E \gets \vec{x}_E + \vec{v}_E \cdot dt$.
\end{enumerate}
The capture condition and the reachable sets of the in-plane parameters
$(\rho, \sigma, \tau)$ are both expressible purely in terms of in-plane
quantities, and replacing any step's action by its in-plane projection (with
the same magnitude budget) never shrinks the actor's reachable set of in-plane
parameters; the claim follows by backward induction over the (finite, under
optimal play) number of steps to capture.  This discrete game reappears as a
numerical scheme in \cref{sec:numerics}.

\section{Formulation as a differential game}
\label{sec:hji}

We formulate the problem as a differential game and employ the
Hamilton--Jacobi--Isaacs (HJI) equation \cite{isaacs1965,evanssouganidis1984}
to obtain a differential equation constraining the value function.

\subsection{The HJI equation}

Let us introduce some notation:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\vec{s}$ is a vector representing the game state, whose elements are
$(s_1, \dots, s_n)$
\item $t$ is time
\item $\phi$ is the pursuer's control
\item $\psi$ is the evader's control
\item $\nabla$ is the vector of partial derivatives $\left(\pdv{}{s_1}, \dots,
\pdv{}{s_n}\right)$
\item $V(\vec{s}, t)$ is the value function of the game
\item $F(\vec{s}, t, \phi, \psi)$ is a function representing the system dynamics
\item $G(\vec{s}, t, \phi, \psi)$ is the cost rate function
\item $H(\vec{s}, t)$ is the bequest value
\end{enumerate}

$F$ represents the dynamics of the system via the first-order differential
equation:
\begin{equation}
\dot{\vec{s}} = F(\vec{s}, \phi, \psi)
\end{equation}

The HJI equation may be written as follows:
\begin{align}
\pdv{V(\vec{s}, t)}{t} + \min_{\phi} \max_{\psi} \left[
  \nabla V(\vec{s}, t) \cdot F(\vec{s}, \phi, \psi) + G(\vec{s}, \phi, \psi)
\right] = 0,
\end{align}
subject to the boundary condition $V(\vec{s}, t) = H(\vec{s}, t)$ on the
target set.  Two standard caveats about the capture formalism: the boundary
condition may only be imposed on the \emph{usable part} of the target set
(those boundary states from which the pursuer can actually force penetration;
cf.\ \cref{sec:regularity}), and $V$ may be nonsmooth, in which case the HJI
equation holds in the viscosity sense
\cite{crandalllions1983,evanssouganidis1984}.  Since our Hamiltonian is
additively separable in $(\phi, \psi)$, the $\min\max$ and $\max\min$
Hamiltonians coincide (Isaacs' condition) and the game has a value.

\subsection{Specializing to the 1M1S game}
\label{sec:reduction}

We specialize the HJI equation for the case of the missile pursuit game.
Here, $V$, $F$, and $G$ lack time dependence, and $H$ is zero.

As established in \cref{sec:symmetries}, the state space is three-dimensional:
\begin{align}
\vec{s} = (\rho, \sigma, \tau),
\end{align}
where $\rho$ is the distance, $\sigma$ is the radial velocity, and $\tau$ is the
tangential speed, and (WLOG) both controls are in-plane:
$\vec a_m = (a_{m\sigma}, a_{m\tau})$ with $\norm{\vec a_m} \le A$ for the
missile and $\vec a_s = (a_{s\sigma}, a_{s\tau})$ with
$\norm{\vec a_s} \le \beta A$ for the spacecraft.  Since $\vec r = \vec x_E -
\vec x_P$, the relative acceleration is $\vec a_s - \vec a_m$, and the system
dynamics are
\begin{align}
\dot{\vec{s}}
&= F(\rho, \sigma, \tau, \vec{a}_m, \vec{a}_s) \notag \\
&= \left(\sigma,\;
  \frac{\tau^2}{\rho} + a_{s\sigma} - a_{m\sigma},\;
  -\frac{\sigma\tau}{\rho} + a_{s\tau} - a_{m\tau}\right).
\end{align}
The terms $\tau^2/\rho$ and $-\sigma\tau/\rho$ are the centrifugal and
Coriolis effects of the rotating line-of-sight frame; equivalently, the
relative angular momentum $h = \rho\tau$ obeys
$\dot h = \rho\,(a_{s\tau} - a_{m\tau})$, so with no thrust $h$ is conserved.
(Note that flipping the sign convention to $\vec a_m - \vec a_s$ leaves the
scalar PDE below unchanged, by the symmetry of the norm balls; it only flips
the optimal control directions.)

We will also write $\vec{a}_m = \phi$ for the missile's control and $\vec{a}_s =
\psi$ for the spacecraft's control.

Substituting all of this, the HJI equation for this game is:
\begin{align}
\min_{\vec{a}_m} \max_{\vec{a}_s} \left[
  \pdv{V}{\rho} \sigma
+ \pdv{V}{\sigma} \left(\frac{\tau^2}{\rho} + a_{s\sigma} - a_{m\sigma}\right)
+ \pdv{V}{\tau} \left(-\frac{\sigma\tau}{\rho} + a_{s\tau} - a_{m\tau}\right)
+ \norm{\vec{a}_m}
\right] = 0 .
\end{align}
The inner optimizations can be carried out in closed form.  Writing
$q \eqdef \sqrt{V_\sigma^2 + V_\tau^2} = \norm{\vec \lambda}$, the maximum
over $\vec a_s$ contributes $+\beta A q$, and the minimum over $\vec a_m$
contributes $-A \max(q - 1, 0)$, yielding the scalar PDE
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:hji-reduced}
  \sigma \pdv{V}{\rho}
  + \frac{\tau^2}{\rho} \pdv{V}{\sigma}
  - \frac{\sigma\tau}{\rho} \pdv{V}{\tau}
  + \beta A q - A \max(q - 1,\, 0) = 0 ,
\end{equation}
which is exactly \cref{eq:defining} expressed in the reduced coordinates.

\subsection{Reduction to two dimensionless variables}

Substituting the scaling form \cref{eq:V-scaling},
$V = \sqrt{A\rho}\, g(u, w)$ with $u = \sigma/\sqrt{A\rho}$,
$w = \tau/\sqrt{A\rho}$, we have
\[
  \pdv{V}{\sigma} = g_u, \qquad
  \pdv{V}{\tau} = g_w, \qquad
  \pdv{V}{\rho} = \frac{A}{2\sqrt{A\rho}} \big( g - u g_u - w g_w \big),
\]
and \cref{eq:hji-reduced} becomes (the dimensional factor $A$ cancels
throughout):
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:g-pde}
  \boxed{\ \frac{u}{2}\big( g - u g_u - w g_w \big)
  + w^2 g_u - u w g_w
  + \beta q - \max( q - 1, \, 0 ) = 0, \qquad q = \sqrt{g_u^2 + g_w^2}. \ }
\end{equation}
This is a first-order PDE in two variables on the half-plane $w \ge 0$ (with
$g$ even in $w$, so $g_w = 0$ on the axis $w = 0$), with $\beta$ as the only
parameter.  The boundary conditions live at infinity in these coordinates:
\begin{itemize}
\item Fast closing ($u \to -\infty$, $w$ bounded): the analysis of
  \cref{sec:coast} gives $g(u, w) \to 0$, at rate $g = \Theta_\beta(\beta /
  \abs{u})$; the coast region $q < 1$ contains a neighborhood of this limit.
\item Fast receding ($u \to +\infty$): the missile must first cancel the
  recession against opposition, so $g$ grows linearly, $g = \Theta_\beta(u)$.
\item From rest ($u = w = 0$): the acceleration-matching policy of
  \cref{sec:matching} gives the upper bound $g(0,0) \le 2\sqrt{\beta}$ (in the
  impulsive-boost idealization; finite missile thrust adds $O(\beta)$
  corrections).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Numerical solution}
\label{sec:numerics}

Two features of \cref{eq:g-pde} bear on numerics.  First, within the model the
accelerations are bounded by $A$, so the characteristics have finite speed and
the standard first-order Taylor expansion underlying the HJB/HJI derivation is
valid; the worry that ``the missile acceleration may be effectively
infinite'' applies only to the \emph{impulsive-boost idealization} ($A \to
\infty$ during a boost), whose proper formalization is an impulse-control
quasi-variational inequality rather than \cref{eq:hji-reduced}.  Second, $V$
is nonsmooth (free boundary; singular surfaces), so a convergent scheme should
be a monotone/upwind scheme for viscosity solutions.

A natural such scheme is the semi-Lagrangian discretization, which is
precisely Bellman's \emph{discrete} principle of optimality applied to the
discrete game of \cref{sec:symmetries}: for a given state $\vec s$, the value
must be the minmax value over states accessible after a single timestep,
\begin{align}
V(\vec{s}) = \min_{\vec{a}_m} \max_{\vec{a}_s} \left[
  V\big(\vec{s}_\text{next}(\vec{a}_m, \vec{a}_s, dt)\big) +
  \norm{\vec{a}_m} \, dt
\right],
\end{align}
where $\vec{s}_\text{next} = \vec{s} + F(\vec s, \vec a_m, \vec a_s)\, dt$ (or
a higher-order step), iterated to a fixed point on a truncated $(u, w)$ grid
with the asymptotics above as far-field conditions.  We leave the numerical
solution of \cref{eq:g-pde}, and in particular the computed shape of the free
boundary $q = 1$, to future work.

\section{Analysis of acceleration matching}
\label{sec:matching}

Acceleration matching is an intuitive strategy that has been proposed and
analyzed previously, though to our knowledge only informally
(\cref{sec:related}); it is closely related to the classical
pursuit-guidance literature (see, e.g., \cite{zarchan2012,ho1965}). Here we
recapitulate the analysis. We repeat the derivation of the optimal allocation
of $\Delta$v between the boost and terminal stages, and the maximum effective
range of this missile assuming a $\Delta$v limit.

In the acceleration matching strategy, missile behavior has two stages:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Boost phase, in which the missile instantaneously gains some velocity
$v_\text{boost}$ toward the target.  (This is the impulsive idealization of a
short full-throttle burn; it is here that finite $A$ is idealized away.)
\item Terminal phase, in which the missile accelerates to match target
accelerations. In this stage the missile has some $\Delta$v budget
$v_\text{term}$.
\end{enumerate}

Consider the situation where the missile and target begin at time $t = 0$ at a
distance $D$ apart and 0 relative velocity. Assume the target has an
acceleration capacity $a$ (in the notation of the preceding sections, $a =
\beta A$, and matching is feasible since $a \le A$).

In acceleration matching, during the terminal phase, the missile maintains a
constant closure velocity $v_\text{boost}$, since it thrusts to cancel the
target acceleration. The time to reach the target $t_\text{hit}$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
t_\text{hit} = \frac{D}{v_\text{boost}}
\end{equation}

The missile is defeated if it runs out of $\Delta$v before reaching the target.
If the target maintains full acceleration $a$ in any direction, the missile
$\Delta$v budget runs out in time $t_\text{defeat}$ given by:
\begin{equation}
t_\text{defeat} = \frac{v_\text{term}}{a}
\end{equation}

In order to hit, we require $t_\text{hit} \le t_\text{defeat}$. Using this, we
can derive the maximum distance $D$ at which the missile may hit the target
given $v_\text{boost}$, $v_\text{term}$, and $a$:
\begin{align}
\frac{D}{v_\text{boost}} &\le \frac{v_\text{term}}{a} \\ \notag
D &\le \frac{v_\text{term} v_\text{boost}}{a}
\end{align}

Given a total $\Delta$v budget $v = v_\text{boost} + v_\text{term}$, we may
select an apportionment that maximizes $D$ by maximizing a quadratic:
\begin{align}
v_\text{boost}^* &= \argmax_{v_\text{boost}}
    \frac{v_\text{boost} (v - v_\text{boost})}{a} \\
v_\text{boost}^* &= \frac{v}{2}
\end{align}

Substituting, we obtain the maximum hit distance $D^*$ for a given total
$\Delta$v budget $v$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:matching-envelope}
D^* = \frac{v^2}{4a}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, capture from rest at distance $D$ costs this policy at most $v =
2\sqrt{aD} = 2\sqrt{\beta A D}$, which is the source of the bound $g(0,0) \le
2\sqrt\beta$ quoted in \cref{sec:reduction}.

\section{Pure acceleration matching is suboptimal in the terminal phase}
\label{sec:lateral-suboptimal}

We now give a simple argument that the \emph{policy} ``always match target
acceleration during the terminal phase'' is dominated: whenever the closure
speed is low relative to the range, converting delta-V into additional
\emph{longitudinal} (along the line of sight) velocity yields a longer
effective range than reserving it for matching.  Interestingly, the envelope
\cref{eq:matching-envelope} survives unchanged from rest -- the classical
optimal split is exactly the point where the two policies coincide -- but from
every other state the refined policy is strictly better.

\paragraph{Exchange rate between longitudinal and matching delta-V.}
Consider a terminal-phase state with range $\rho$, closure speed $\sigma > 0$
(we take $\sigma$ positive-closing in this section for readability), remaining
budget $w$, and target acceleration capacity $a$.  The matching policy's
cost-to-go is
\[
  J_\text{match}(\rho, \sigma) = a \, t_{go} = \frac{a \rho}{\sigma} .
\]
A marginal unit of delta-V spent longitudinally instead increases $\sigma$ and
saves
\[
  -\pdv{J_\text{match}}{\sigma} = \frac{a\rho}{\sigma^2}
\]
units of future matching cost.  The exchange is favorable exactly when
\[
  \frac{a\rho}{\sigma^2} > 1
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \sigma < \sqrt{a\rho} .
\]
In dimensionless terms the switching locus is $\sigma^2 / (a \rho) = 1$ -- the
same dimensionless group $\Pi$ singled out in \cref{sec:symmetries}, and a
heuristic shadow of the free boundary $q = 1$ of \cref{eq:g-pde}.

\paragraph{The refined policy and its envelope.}
This suggests the policy: \emph{if $\sigma < \sqrt{a\rho}$, boost
  (longitudinally) up to closure $\sqrt{a\rho}$, then match}.  Its cost-to-go
is
\[
  J_\text{refined}(\rho, \sigma) =
  \begin{cases}
    2\sqrt{a\rho} - \sigma, & \sigma \le \sqrt{a\rho}, \\[2pt]
    \dfrac{a\rho}{\sigma}, & \sigma \ge \sqrt{a\rho},
  \end{cases}
\]
(boost cost $\sqrt{a\rho} - \sigma$ plus matching cost
$a\rho/\sqrt{a\rho} = \sqrt{a\rho}$; once $\sigma \ge \sqrt{a\rho}$ the state
stays on the matching side, since $\sigma$ is held constant while $\rho$
shrinks).  By the AM--GM inequality,
\[
  \frac{a\rho}{\sigma} + \sigma \ \ge\ 2\sqrt{a\rho}
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  J_\text{refined} \le J_\text{match},
\]
with equality iff $\sigma = \sqrt{a\rho}$: pure matching is strictly dominated
everywhere below the switching locus.  In terms of effective range from a
state $(\sigma, w)$: pure matching reaches targets out to
\[
  D_\text{match} = \frac{w \sigma}{a},
  \qquad\text{while}\qquad
  D_\text{refined} = \frac{(w + \sigma)^2}{4a},
\]
and $(w+\sigma)^2 \ge 4 w \sigma$ gives $D_\text{refined} \ge D_\text{match}$,
with equality iff $w = \sigma$ -- i.e., iff the remaining budget exactly
equals the current closure speed.

\paragraph{Why the classical envelope is nevertheless unchanged from rest.}
Launching from rest with total budget $v$ and boosting by $\sigma$ leaves
$w = v - \sigma$, so $D_\text{refined} = (w + \sigma)^2/4a = v^2/4a$
\emph{independently of the split}, matching \cref{eq:matching-envelope}.  The
classical optimal split $v_\text{boost}^* = v/2$ is precisely the choice that
makes $w = \sigma$: it places the missile exactly on the switching locus
$\sigma = \sqrt{a D^*}$ (indeed $\sqrt{a \cdot v^2/4a} = v/2$), after which
matching keeps it on the matched side for the rest of the flight.  So the
classical analysis obtains the right envelope from rest, but for a
non-obvious reason; and the always-match policy is fragile.  From any
off-nominal state -- a mis-sized boost, an engagement inherited with existing
closure, or mid-course radial evasion that has eroded $\sigma$ -- re-boosting
per the rule $\sigma < \sqrt{a\rho}$ strictly extends the capture envelope,
by the factor $(w+\sigma)^2 / 4w\sigma$.

\paragraph{What this does and does not show.}
The refined policy improves only the longitudinal channel; it retains
instant lateral matching.  A lever-arm argument suggests lateral matching is
in fact locally optimal within the linearized lateral subgame: the target
purchases predicted miss (zero-effort miss \cite{zarchan2012}) at rate
proportional to the current time-to-go, and the missile's price to remove it
only grows as time-to-go shrinks, so postponing corrections is dominated,
while pre-empting them is punished by a target reversal.  We do not make this
rigorous here; the exact optimal blend of longitudinal and lateral effort --
and the true constant $g(0,0) \le 2\sqrt\beta$, i.e., whether the matching
envelope constant $1/4$ in \cref{eq:matching-envelope} is game-optimal -- is
determined by the solution of \cref{eq:g-pde}, which we leave to future
(numerical) work.

\section{Open questions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Solve \cref{eq:g-pde} numerically; determine the free boundary $q = 1$
  and the from-rest constant $g(0,0;\beta)$, and compare against
  $2\sqrt\beta$.
\item Prove a comparison/uniqueness theorem adapted to the discontinuous
  boundary data on the non-usable part of the target set (or regularize with a
  capture radius $\ell$ and control the limit $\ell \to 0$).
\item Sharpen \cref{thm:finite-dv}'s dependence on $(1-\beta)$ and determine
  the asymptotics of $V$ as $\beta \to 1$.
\item Characterize the singular surfaces of $V$ (where the saddle point of
  \cref{sec:equilibrium} is non-unique) and the structure of optimal
  trajectories crossing them.
\end{enumerate}
